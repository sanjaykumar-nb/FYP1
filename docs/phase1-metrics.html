1 · Correctness

Does each agent compute exactly what it claims?

Every check below ran on every real-sprint analysis. The Planning, Progress and Workload figures were compared with an independent recount from the raw sprint, not with the agents' own code.

Each detector changes its verdict exactly at its documented threshold

Severity as the measured signal is swept across its range on generated projects. The vertical rule marks the threshold the documentation states.

Show the numbers
What it shows
Across 2,961 analyses nothing failed: every agent completed, every output matched its schema, the same sprint always produced the same answer, and all 40,001 tasks and people cited as evidence exist in that sprint's graph. On 180 generated projects with a planted problem, detection is exact for all six risk types (F1 1.00), and each detector switches at its documented value.
You can claim
The agents are correct and deterministic: they report the true figures for the data they are given, their evidence is never invented, and their thresholds behave as documented.
Don't claim
That this makes the verdicts useful. Correct computation of a signal is not the same as that signal predicting anything — section 2 measures that separately.

What it shows
Halfway through a sprint the risk agent reaches F1 0.706, rising to 0.756 at three-quarters (0.803 counting only high-or-worse warnings). Against flagging every sprint the gains are +0.050 and +0.099, and both intervals stay clear of zero (p < 0.001). It ranks late sprints well — AUC 0.792 and 0.874 — but no better than simply counting open work. The tuned model scores 0.816 on the projects it was tuned on and falls to 0.593 on unseen ones.
You can claim
The system warns about late sprints while they are still running, significantly better than flagging everything, on projects it has never seen. Added model complexity did not survive the project boundary: the tuned model's advantage disappeared.
Don't claim
That the system beats the tuned model — at sprint end the two cannot be separated (p = 0.78, the interval crosses zero). That its projection ranks better than counting open work — it does not. That sprint-end recall of 1.00 is skill — after the deadline, the label and the rule test the same condition.
2b · Every agent's own verdict

Do the specialist agents' warnings mean anything on their own?

Planning, Progress and Workload each give a risk level of their own, shown to users beside the Risk agent's. They do not feed the product's risk score, but they are scored here against the same outcome: did the sprint finish late?

F1 of each agent's warning, held-out sprints

A warning is any risk level above low. 270 sprints from 22 unseen projects.

Risk agent — the product's verdictSpecialist agents

Show the numbers
What it shows
The Planning agent now warns when a sprint plans more story points than the team can finish — the capacity set in project settings, or else the average its last three finished sprints completed. (Before this, no capacity ever reached it and it never warned.) Its warning is weak: F1 0.500 halfway, below flagging every sprint (0.657), because most teams here plan more than they finish; the plan-to-capacity ratio ranks late sprints above on-time ones with AUC 0.60, against 0.79 for the Risk agent. The Progress agent's simple completion rule matches the Risk agent halfway through (F1 0.712 vs 0.706); at sprint end its perfect precision is built in, since a sprint under half done is late by definition. The Workload agent is a weak lateness signal (F1 0.58) — it measures uneven load, not delay.
You can claim
The product's verdict rests on the Risk agent, which is the one validated here; the specialists supply descriptive context whose figures are exactly right (section 1), the Planning agent's capacity and plan included.
Don't claim
That the Planning agent predicts late sprints: it is a planning check, and on this data a weak one. Its threshold was fixed in advance, not tuned on these sprints. Nor that the pace projection beats a plain completion-rate rule halfway through: they perform the same.
3 · Suggested fixes

Do the reassignments it suggests actually work?

For every real sprint with a workload recommendation, each suggested move was applied to the sprint and the risk recomputed, then compared with what the recommendation predicted before anyone acted.

Where workload risk ended after the suggested moves

1,524 sprints, grouped by the severity they started at. The planner makes at most three moves.

Show the numbers

Recommendations made, by risk type

Every medium-or-worse risk across the 1,974 real-sprint analyses got one: 4,363 of 4,363.

Show the numbers
What it shows
The predicted outcome held in every one of 1,524 cases — both the new heaviest load and the new severity. Severity dropped at least one level in 62%; the heaviest load fell by a median of 6 story points. Most of the rest started critical: 519 teams were too uneven for three moves to lift. In 284 sprints no move could help, and the recommendation says so rather than suggesting one.
You can claim
The fixes are actionable and honest: each one names concrete moves, and its predicted effect is exactly what applying them produces.
Don't claim
That the fixes improve real projects — this is measured inside the model, not with teams who followed them. And knowledge and coordination risk never reached medium on a real sprint, so their recommendations are exercised on generated data only.

Analysis time by sprint size

The agent pipeline alone, in process, across 1,974 real-sprint analyses.

Show the numbers
What it shows
As the project grows from 13 to 2,003 tasks, the prompt grows only from 266 to 390 tokens (1.47×), while sending the whole project grows to 189,834 — 487× larger (515× re-measured today, as tasks gained two fields). Every page and edit answers in 8–42 ms; a full analysis, backend and AI service together, takes 715 ms, of which the agent pipeline takes under 50 ms (22–37 ms median in the latest run, by sprint size). Sign-in's 390 ms is deliberate: password hashing is made slow to resist guessing. Under load, with up to 50 people at once and no pause between requests, the server peaks near 78 requests a second with no failed requests and the slowest 1% within 1.4 s — after fixing three defects the load test itself found (7 failed requests before; 25 simultaneous sign-ins took 9.5 s).
You can claim
Prompt cost tracks the number of problems, not project size, and the product is fast enough to re-analyse on every change: everything but a full analysis responds well inside the 100 ms that feels instant, and 50 simultaneous users cause waits, not errors.
Don't claim
Production-scale performance. These times and the load test are from one laptop on SQLite with a 30-task project, the load generator on the same machine; CI repeats the load test on PostgreSQL on a shared runner. Token counts are estimates from character length, not a tokenizer.
5 · The optional language model

Is the language model safe to put in front of users?

The model is optional and only puts findings into words. These results come from the published runs on a rate-limited free key; re-measuring them needs a paid key.

How often the model's output was valid

Only the end-to-end rate is measured. The model's own rate is bounded, because only 24 of 50 failures were diagnosed individually.

Measured end to end (rate limits included)Model's own validity: possible range
Show the numbers
What it shows
Every fabricated citation injected in testing — 200 of 200 — was removed before reaching the user. The model's verdict agrees with the deterministic one almost perfectly (κ 0.95). Only 41.1% of calls produced valid output end to end, mostly because of free-tier rate limits. Without a key the model is never called: 0 of 8,883 attempts reached the network.
You can claim
The model narrates, it does not re-decide, and it cannot put invented evidence in front of a user. The product loses nothing without it.
Don't claim
A validity rate for the model itself — it is a range, 54–95%. The κ of 0.95 rests on 37 comparisons, with three risk types at a single one; treat it as a first signal, not a settled number.

What it shows

Limits of the data behind these numbers

  • The delay label is a proxy. TAWOS has no due dates, so "late" means 30% or more of a sprint's issues unresolved at its end.
  • The first quarter of a sprint. The pace warning is off by design until a quarter of the schedule has passed.
  • One machine. Response times and the load test come from one laptop, on SQLite, with a 30-task project; CI repeats the load test on PostgreSQL.
  • The language-model results are from earlier runs on a rate-limited free key; they were not re-measured.
